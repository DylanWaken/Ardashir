A sunlit courtyard looks simple from the player's point of view. Stone walls surround a tree. A guard crosses the paving, a wooden crate moves into the shade, and a hanging lamp warms the wall beside it. The renderer has to answer two questions before this picture exists: which surface is visible at every pixel, and how much light leaves that surface toward the camera? Nanite changes the cost and organization of the first question. Lumen makes an approximation to the second question practical when geometry and illumination can change.

The difficult part is that neither question can be answered by keeping one perfect copy of the world and inspecting all of it every frame. A detailed stone wall contains far more triangles than the picture contains pixels. Indirect lighting potentially connects every surface to every other surface. Both systems therefore construct representations that preserve useful answers while discarding, delaying, or sharing work. Understanding exactly what is preserved is more useful than memorizing feature names.

This article develops the machinery from first principles, then follows it into Unreal Engine's implementation. It assumes ordinary programming knowledge, but introduces the graphics concepts it uses. Its source baseline is the local Unreal Engine 5.8.1 checkout at commit 71fe36aac5a8df5ccd66c763ffc902b29b6a9c43. Source links point to that revision and require access to Epic's Unreal repository. Defaults quoted below are C++ initialization values in this checkout; project settings, platform profiles, scalability settings, and runtime changes can override them. The 2021 Nanite presentation by Brian Karis, Rune Stubbe, and Graham Wihlidal supplies the historical starting point, while the code supplies the boundaries of present implementation. The courtyard is a constructed teaching example, not a benchmark or an Unreal sample project.

1. Begin with the picture, then work backward to the mesh

A triangle mesh is an indexed list of positions together with attributes such as surface normals and texture coordinates. Three indices identify a triangle. An instance places this reusable geometry in the world with a transform: a rule that moves, rotates, and scales its local coordinates. The camera then transforms world positions into a projected image. Rasterization determines which pixel samples a projected triangle covers. A material program evaluates the surface properties needed to shade those samples.

In a conventional draw, the CPU arranges vertex buffers, index buffers, material bindings, and a draw command. The GPU transforms vertices, assembles triangles, rasterizes their coverage, and runs shaders. This organization is excellent when each triangle earns its cost by covering a substantial piece of the picture. It becomes awkward when a scanned stone wall has millions of triangles and is only a few hundred pixels wide. Most triangles contribute less than one pixel, yet conventional submission still describes the original geometry. Batching many objects reduces CPU commands without solving the GPU's excess triangle work.

Consider a deliberately simplified numerical example. A wall occupies 800 by 400 pixels, an area of 320,000 pixel locations. Its source mesh contains 8 million triangles. If all triangles are submitted, there are 25 source triangles for each pixel location before counting overdraw, hidden faces, and other views. The picture cannot display all those independently resolved shapes. This does not prove that exactly 320,000 triangles suffice: silhouettes, sharp changes of depth, perspective, and sampling complicate that statement. It does show why cost proportional to source triangle count is a poor objective.

A traditional level of detail, or LOD, replaces the whole mesh with a simpler mesh when it is farther away. That already changes the objective toward image complexity. However, a large courtyard wall can stretch from the camera to the far corner. One global LOD either overspends on distant regions or simplifies the near region too aggressively. A better representation allows different portions of a single mesh to choose different resolutions. To make that economical, those portions must also be independently culled, stored, and requested.

Nanite's central decision is to preprocess geometry into small, bounded units and several related approximations, then choose the visible approximation on the GPU. This moves a large part of per-object, per-LOD decision making away from CPU draw submission. It does not make the original mesh disappear: the build process still has to read it, derive the representation, store it, and sometimes retain alternative geometry for other consumers. Virtual geometry is a change in runtime access and granularity, not an assertion of infinite storage.

The runtime also has more than the main camera to satisfy. Shadows need views from lights. Lumen's surface captures need views of materials from directions that expose surfaces to a cache. A solution that works only for one camera would leave much of a modern frame expensive. Nanite therefore treats a view as explicit input to selection and rasterization. Different views can ask for different detail from the same shared asset. The important reusable object is the geometry representation; the selection is temporary work for a particular view.

The distinction between geometry and appearance will remain important throughout this article. A flat wall covered by an expensive layered material can have few triangles and still shade slowly. A highly detailed unlit statue can have many source triangles and shade cheaply after selection. Nanite mainly reorganizes geometry processing and visibility, while still accommodating material work. Lumen consumes geometry and material information to approximate light transport. Neither subsystem can be assigned a useful performance budget using polygon count alone.

2. Small clusters are useful only if their boundaries remain compatible

The first step toward local LOD is to partition a mesh into clusters. A cluster is a bounded group of nearby geometry with metadata that makes it manageable as a unit. For the triangle path in the inspected engine, shared definitions encode a maximum of 128 triangles and 256 vertices per cluster. Those numbers describe capacity, not a promise that every cluster is exactly full. The limit keeps work and storage bounded and makes it possible to organize GPU processing around a known upper size. The source establishes these constants in NaniteDefinitions.h.

Suppose the wall is initially divided into four adjacent clusters, A through D. To make a coarser version, one might independently remove vertices from each cluster until it has half as many triangles. But two neighboring clusters share a geometric boundary. If A changes that boundary and B does not, their surfaces no longer meet. The renderer has created a hole even though each cluster's internal simplification looked reasonable. This is a representation correctness problem, not an antialiasing problem.

The obvious repair is to freeze every vertex on every cluster boundary. Now independent simplification cannot pull those shared edges apart. Unfortunately, a hierarchy that preserves the same boundaries forever eventually spends most of its coarse triangles representing boundaries that should have vanished. Imagine a long line of tiny stone chips lying along the original partition between A and B. At a distance where the entire wall should be nearly flat, the frozen partition still demands enough vertices to preserve all those chips.

Nanite's construction solves this by simplifying groups of clusters together. Inside one group, old boundaries become interior edges and can be simplified. The group's outside boundary stays constrained so that it remains compatible with its neighbors. The simplified result is partitioned into new clusters, and the next grouping can put an old outer boundary inside a new group. This alternation prevents an arbitrary original partition from becoming a permanent geometric obligation.

The current builder exposes this reasoning directly. FCluster::SimplifyTriangles walks external edges and locks their endpoint positions before reduction. FClusterDAG::ReduceGroup merges a group's children, simplifies the result, splits it back into bounded clusters, and assigns the parents shared LOD data. These are not incidental class names: they reveal the construction invariant that the runtime will later depend on. See the external-edge constraints and the group reduction and parent metadata.

A simplifier also has to account for more than position. The wall's texture coordinates identify where a brick texture is sampled; its normals control reflected light; material boundaries can separate stone from metal. A triangle reduction that minimizes only positional displacement can ruin appearance. In practice, the builder has attribute-aware costs and settings, and some data cannot be interpolated as though it were an ordinary continuous coordinate. This is why authoring semantics still matter even in an automatic geometry pipeline.

Each coarse result records an error measure relating it to the finer geometry. Conceptually, this is a bound or conservative estimate of the spatial deviation introduced by replacement. The runtime projects that error into the view to decide whether the replacement is acceptable. The current reduction propagates child errors upward and forces parent error values to respect the grouped construction. Its implementation explicitly takes maxima while building the parent data. Without that monotonic relationship, traversal could accept a coarse approximation that claims to be more accurate than a descendant despite having discarded more information.

The triangle explanation is the simplest entrance into Nanite, but this engine checkout contains additional curve and voxel shape-preservation branches in the same builder. Their existence is visible in ReduceGroup; it is not evidence that every content type on every platform automatically uses those paths. The stable lesson is the build/runtime contract: the build stores a hierarchy of compatible alternatives with error and bounds, and runtime selection must obey those relationships. When reading newer Nanite code, preserve that contract while checking which geometry branch the asset actually built.

3. A level of detail becomes a valid cut through a graph

Grouping and repartitioning change the shape of the hierarchy. A simple tree gives each child one parent. Here, several coarse clusters jointly replace the same group of finer clusters. A fine cluster can therefore participate under more than one coarse cluster in the dependency relation. The result is a directed acyclic graph, or DAG: edges point from one resolution toward another, paths can share descendants, and the build does not create cycles that lead back to the same resolution.

There is a practical difference between saying “a hierarchy” and understanding this graph. In a tree, drawing one parent instead of its children is locally straightforward. In the Nanite graph, a coarser representation may consist of a collection of parents that must agree about the replacement. Drawing one coarse parent and some arbitrary subset of its finer shared descendants can duplicate geometry or omit it. The valid state is a cut: a set of selected alternatives that covers the required surface once while respecting the construction dependencies.

To understand the view test, use a small-angle approximation. If an approximation has world-space error e, lies about distance z along the view, and the projection has focal scale f measured in pixels, the projected error is roughly f e / z. With f equal to 1,000 pixels and e equal to 0.002 meters, the error is about one pixel at two meters and one tenth of a pixel at twenty meters. These are explanatory numbers, not Unreal's complete culling equation.

The complete implementation must deal with bounding volumes, nonuniform scale, perspective, orthographic views, deformation, and conservative estimates over an entire cluster. It does not merely evaluate the distance from the camera to one vertex. In NaniteClusterCulling.usf, hierarchy traversal compares projected scales with stored LOD errors and then chooses raster work. The view includes its own LOD scale. The source's use of bounds and scale factors is the implementation of the same geometric idea, adapted to cases that a one-line formula leaves out.

For a wall stretching away from the camera, near groups can select finer replacements while far groups remain coarse. The grouping constraints limit how independently these choices can happen, but they still allow much finer control than one LOD for the entire wall. A shadow view may select differently from the camera because its projected footprint and quality target differ. Thus “the mesh is at LOD 2” is usually the wrong description of a Nanite frame. A more accurate description is that a particular view selected a set of clusters.

The hierarchy used to accelerate traversal should also be distinguished from the dependency graph used to define compatible simplification. Bounds let the GPU reject regions without visiting every cluster. Group relationships define which geometry can replace which. They cooperate, but they answer different questions. An engineer implementing a similar system can use one data structure to encode both compactly, yet should keep the logical responsibilities separate. A bug in traversal bounds causes missing or excess work; a bug in replacement dependencies can create discontinuities even when every visibility test is correct.

Finally, an acceptable error does not mean all differences become invisible. A projected threshold is an engineering control, and visibility depends on motion, silhouette, material, contrast, and temporal reconstruction. The source default of r.Nanite.MaxPixelsPerEdge is one, while other settings influence hardware raster thresholds and dynamic scaling. These values are quality and scheduling controls, not a proof that geometry error is exactly one pixel everywhere. The current controls make that distinction explicit through separate LOD and rasterization parameters.

4. The same hierarchy makes geometry demand-paged

Once a coarse representation can stand in for finer geometry, the finer data no longer needs to remain resident merely to keep the object drawable. The engine can retain a coarse resident representation and request additional detail when a view needs it. This is the virtual part of virtualized geometry: an asset has a logical addressable representation larger than the detail currently present in GPU memory.

Storage is organized into pages, which are transfer and residency units containing encoded cluster data and associated information. A cluster is a geometry-processing unit; a page is a memory-management unit. Confusing the two produces bad accounting. One page can carry several clusters, and making one desired cluster available can involve loading data that shares its page. Current shared constants reserve 32 KiB for root GPU pages and 128 KiB for streaming GPU pages. These are GPU page sizes in this source revision, not a prediction of an asset's compressed disk size. The definitions distinguish them.

Encoding the page is part of the design rather than a final compression checkbox. Vertex positions can be quantized onto a grid: instead of storing unrestricted floating-point values, store integers plus the information needed to reconstruct positions at a chosen precision. A cluster whose coordinates occupy a small range can use fewer bits for that range. But two clusters that meet at a boundary must reconstruct compatible positions; independently rounding the same point by incompatible rules could reopen the cracks that simplification carefully avoided. The current uniform-grid position quantization makes the precision decision explicit.

Other attributes have different redundancy. A unit-length normal lies on a sphere, so three unconstrained floating-point numbers spend more information than the direction fundamentally needs. Octahedral encoding maps that direction to a compact two-dimensional representation; the current normal encoding routines then quantize its coordinates. Texture coordinates, material information, and triangle connectivity need their own encodings because their semantics differ. Reducing bytes therefore involves several field-specific choices, each with quality and decoding costs, rather than one generic compression ratio applied to a raw vertex buffer.

The storage-friendly page and the GPU-consumable page need not have identical layout. Transcoding converts the streamed representation into the form that runtime decoding expects. Unreal's page uploader declares GPU transcode work with independent and parent-dependent passes. This makes page dependencies operational: some decoding work relies on related information being available. When estimating memory and bandwidth, distinguish source asset size, cooked disk data, staging transfers, resident encoded GPU pages, and temporary decoding or installation work. A reduction in one number does not automatically imply the same reduction in every other number.

Imagine approaching the far wall. The GPU visits the hierarchy and discovers that the resident coarse cut is no longer detailed enough. It emits requests for finer pages. The CPU-side streaming manager receives feedback, schedules reads, prepares data, installs ready pages, and updates references so that subsequent traversal can use them. The camera's current frame cannot wait for an arbitrary storage request to complete. It continues to draw a valid resident representation while the requested detail is in flight.

Page installation is more subtle than copying a byte range. Encoded hierarchy references and cluster dependencies must become valid together. A pointer into an evicted page cannot remain available to traversal, and newly installed descendants cannot be selected before the required dependency information is consistent. Unreal's streaming manager therefore has explicit pending-page queues, installation work, root-page accounting, and hierarchy updates. The streaming configuration and initialization show bounded pending requests and per-update installation limits rather than an unbounded “load everything requested” operation.

In the inspected initialization, the streaming pool is 512 MiB, maximum pending pages is 128, and maximum page installations per frame is 128. They should be read as default controls of this checkout, not universally appropriate project settings. More memory can reduce eviction and reload traffic, but it cannot fix an asset with pathological geometry distribution. A larger installation allowance can help a fast camera catch up, but it moves decompression, upload, and update work into a more concentrated frame budget.

Residency constraints and visual error constraints are related without being identical. The ideal cut for a view describes the detail the picture would benefit from. The available cut describes what can be safely selected from installed data. When the camera teleports, these may differ sharply. The user sees detail refine over time even if rendering remains stable. When a hierarchy is poorly built or dependencies are mishandled, the user may instead see holes. Those are different failures and should have different debugging tools.

This is also why Nanite streaming should not be confused with Unreal's fallback mesh. A coarser resident Nanite representation participates in Nanite's own hierarchy. A fallback mesh is an alternate conventional geometry representation used by consumers or platforms that do not use the full Nanite path. Later, hardware ray tracing may intersect such a fallback mesh while the camera displays Nanite clusters. The existence of both mechanisms is necessary, but calling them both “fallback LOD” hides the fact that they live in different rendering systems.

For a future implementation, a useful test is a deliberately slow storage path. Move the camera quickly while constraining page installs and ensure the object remains geometrically complete. Then raise the memory budget and repeat to distinguish bandwidth pressure from residency pressure. This test asks a stronger question than “does the screenshot look right once everything has loaded?” It verifies that the representation remains coherent while its physical storage changes.

5. Let the GPU remove work before rasterization

A high-detail representation is only affordable if most of it is rejected before expensive processing. The GPU begins with instances and their bounds. The camera's view volume, or frustum, defines the region that can project into the image. An instance entirely outside that volume can be discarded. For surviving instances, traversal descends through bounds until it finds the appropriate visible clusters. This coarse-to-fine organization makes rejection proportional to hierarchy work rather than to every triangle in every asset.

Bounds are promises. If a box says it contains the waving leaves but the material displaces them outside it, a correct culler can reject geometry that the player should see. If the box is excessively inflated, correctness is preserved but much less work can be removed. Deformation support therefore has a culling cost even before a single displaced vertex is evaluated. This is one reason a vertex animation feature cannot be bolted onto an otherwise static renderer by modifying only the vertex shader.

Occlusion adds another opportunity. A stone arch may hide most of the courtyard behind it. After rendering depth, the renderer can build a hierarchical depth buffer, usually abbreviated HZB. Each level summarizes a larger region of the screen. A projected bound can be tested against a suitably coarse depth level; if the depth information conservatively establishes that the bound is behind already visible surfaces, the enclosed geometry need not be rasterized.

The word conservatively matters. A falsely visible cluster wastes time. A falsely occluded cluster disappears. A depth summary and comparison must respect the engine's depth convention and the entire projected bound. It is not safe to test only the bound's center against one unrelated depth sample. Unreal commonly uses reversed depth, so simplistic “smaller always means nearer” explanations are particularly misleading. The culling shaders encapsulate the appropriate depth tests rather than scattering informal comparisons across materials.

Using only last frame's depth would be dangerous. When the camera steps sideways, a previously hidden crate can become visible. Nanite's two-pass occlusion organization uses previous information for an efficient main pass and retests occluded candidates using updated depth for a post pass. In this checkout, r.Nanite.Culling.TwoPass initializes to one. The C++ control and the main/post branches of instance culling expose the intended recovery mechanism. Turning the feature off for diagnosis is not equivalent to proving that history-based rejection is always safe.

The survivor list is produced on the GPU. This avoids reading a visibility result back to the CPU merely so the CPU can issue one draw for each visible cluster. Instead, counters and indirect argument buffers describe the work to execute. An indirect dispatch or draw takes its dimensions from GPU-written memory. The command stream still exists, and CPU-side orchestration still matters, but fine-grained decisions stay next to the data that made them.

There are limits to what occlusion can accomplish. Dense foliage contains many holes, so a coarse depth summary often cannot prove that anything behind it is fully hidden. Layers of leaves may survive culling even when the final image looks like a compact green mass. Overlapping surfaces and distant disconnected details can therefore remain expensive. Nanite reduces the penalty of tiny triangles; it does not repeal the need to process competing visible candidates before deciding which sample wins.

A useful debugging experiment is to compare a courtyard wall with a similarly sized hedge. Give both similar final screen area and similar material complexity. The solid wall should permit stronger rejection of hidden interior detail and geometry behind it. The hedge may continue to generate substantial raster work because holes and layered coverage prevent conservative elimination. If the hedge is slower, that does not contradict the pixel-based motivation. It identifies a case where resolving the pixels still requires many candidate surfaces.

6. Rasterize identity first, then recover the surface

After selection and culling, a cluster still contains triangles whose screen coverage must be resolved. GPUs have dedicated hardware rasterizers, so why would Nanite also contain a software rasterizer implemented with compute shaders? The answer lies in the distribution of triangle sizes. Fixed-function hardware is extremely effective for sufficiently large triangles, but setup and pixel execution organization can become inefficient for huge numbers of tiny triangles. A specialized compute path can process small triangles with a different work distribution.

Software here means shader instructions running on the GPU, not a CPU drawing pixels one at a time. Nanite can route suitable geometry toward hardware or compute rasterization. Both paths ultimately contribute compatible visibility information. The choice is a scheduling decision informed by projected size and supported behavior, not an artistic property of the mesh. In cluster selection, hardware-raster preference is calculated alongside the LOD test, while the raster controls expose separate thresholds and the compute-rasterization switch.

Traditional opaque rendering often evaluates material properties as a fragment survives rasterization. A visibility-buffer design separates two questions: which primitive won, and what properties does it have? The visibility result stores enough identity to retrieve the winning triangle and the instance that placed it. Depth establishes ordering. Later work decodes geometry, reconstructs attributes, and evaluates the material for the visible surface. The visibility buffer is therefore an address into scene data, not an already shaded photograph.

Think of one pixel on the crate. Several wall, crate, and leaf triangles may cover it. Each candidate has a depth and an identity. The depth test chooses the nearest applicable surface. If the crate wins, later shading needs the crate's triangle vertices, its instance transform, and the material assigned to that triangle. Storing only the crate's base color would lose texture coordinates, material identity, and the information required for richer evaluation. Storing every possible property before resolving visibility would spend bandwidth and shading work on losing surfaces.

The inspected implementation includes a 64-bit visibility path whose pixel write packs an identity with depth and uses an atomic operation to select the winner. The exact packing and reversed-depth comparison are implementation details that must agree across producers and consumers. The pixel-write shader makes this contract concrete. A 64-bit write is not an invitation to hard-code a universal bit allocation into a new engine: platform capabilities and Nanite representations have evolved, while the logical requirement remains a consistent depth-and-identity result.

Reconstructing a point on a triangle introduces barycentric coordinates. For triangle vertices A, B, and C, weights a, b, and c sum to one and describe a point as aA + bB + cC. The same idea interpolates texture coordinates and other vertex attributes, with perspective correction where needed. A visible sample near A gives A a larger weight. These weights are not texture coordinates themselves; they are the recipe for obtaining the texture coordinates associated with that triangle's surface.

Texture filtering requires another piece of information: how those coordinates change across the image. A pixel shader traditionally gets derivatives from nearby shader lanes arranged in screen-space quads. A compute material pass with reordered work cannot blindly assume adjacent threads shade adjacent pixels of the same triangle. Analytic or explicitly reconstructed derivatives preserve the intended texture footprint. Otherwise, the geometry can be perfectly visible while textures shimmer or choose incorrect mip levels. Unreal's barycentric helper implementation and Nanite attribute interpolation show why recovering a surface involves more than loading three positions.

The geometry visibility result finally joins the rest of the opaque renderer through depth and material outputs. This permits Nanite and conventional meshes to coexist in one image. A game can render the stone wall with Nanite, a supported character path with its own deformation system, and translucent glass through another pass. The shared contract is what the later rendering stages can consume, not a requirement that every object use the same triangle submission mechanism.

7. Materials can change coverage, not just color

The visibility-first explanation appears to assume that triangle positions and coverage are already known. A stone material largely fits that assumption. A leaf material does not necessarily fit it. It may move vertices with World Position Offset, abbreviated WPO, and discard samples through an opacity mask. The renderer must execute those parts of the material before it can decide which surface is visible. Evaluating the leaf's color afterward cannot repair a visibility decision made using the undeformed rectangle.

This creates two different reasons to group material work. A raster bin groups compatible behavior needed while establishing geometry and coverage. A shading bin groups the material evaluation performed for visible samples. Two materials can share raster behavior but have different shading behavior. Conversely, a material whose WPO is disabled for one instance may permit a cheaper raster path for that instance even though its surface appearance uses the same material family. Treating a bin as simply “one material” loses these distinctions.

The original 2021 Nanite design presentation describes a much more restricted rasterization context than the contemporary engine. Epic's later Fortnite work introduced programmable rasterization to production content, including masked and vertex-deformed surfaces. The historical images below are from Epic's 2023 article about UE5.1, not captures of the local 5.8.1 build. They are useful because they show the same scene under ordinary appearance and under a raster-work classification.

The current source initializes both compute rasterization and programmable rasterization to enabled. It also includes a runtime tessellation control initialized to enabled, subject to the asset, material, platform, and project support that actually select that path. A global enable is not a guarantee that a particular mesh tessellates. The current controls should be read together with shader permutations and mesh capability checks, rather than treated as a checklist of universally available behavior.

WPO has an additional consequence for deferred shading. When visible attributes are reconstructed later, they must correspond to the same deformed surface used for visibility. Depth, triangle identity, interpolation, and any required current or previous deformation must agree. If the visibility pass bends the leaf but later code reconstructs its attributes from an incompatible position, normal mapping, motion, and lighting can detach from the geometry. Supporting deformation consistently is an obligation across passes.

For masked leaves, the opacity test may require texture access before a winner is known. A dense stack of leaves can therefore execute material-dependent work for several candidates that eventually lose. Visibility-first shading removes much wasted full surface shading, but it cannot defer a calculation needed to establish visibility itself. This explains why replacing alpha-cutout cards with carefully authored opaque leaf geometry can have a different performance profile even when the opaque geometry has more triangles.

After visibility, the current Nanite shading implementation builds bin and dispatch data for the material work. Its output provides the opaque renderer with surface quantities such as normal, base response, roughness, and other shading-model data. A deferred renderer stores such quantities in geometry buffers, usually shortened to GBuffer, so that lights can be evaluated later without re-running the complete authored material graph for every light. The separate material graph compilation article explains how one authored graph becomes these different executable contexts; the essential point here is that visibility and surface evaluation ask different subsets of the graph to run.

Transparency requires another caution. The one-winning-surface model is a good starting point for opaque and masked coverage. Glass can transmit light through multiple surfaces and cannot generally be reduced to the same single opaque depth winner. This engine contains evolving Nanite translucency code, but its presence does not make every blend mode or lighting path equivalent to opaque Nanite. For an actual project, validate the mesh, material blend mode, platform, and chosen translucency path together. The courtyard's glass will be discussed as a distinct transport and compositing problem rather than silently folded into opaque visibility.

8. A shadow is another visibility problem with a different observer

Direct lighting asks how much light reaches a visible surface from a light source. For a simple point-like light, one part of the answer is whether an occluder lies between light and surface. A shadow map answers this by rendering depth from the light's viewpoint. During lighting, the receiver is transformed into that view and its depth is compared with the stored nearest depth. The shadow-map view has its own projection and sampling resolution; it is not the camera image reused from a different angle.

Virtual Shadow Maps divide that depth representation into pages so that only needed regions consume physical storage and rendering work. The word virtual here follows a familiar idea: the logical shadow resolution can be large while the physically mapped pages are demand-driven. Nanite is particularly useful when rendering those requested pages because it can choose and cull geometry for the shadow views at fine granularity. But the two systems own different pages. A Nanite geometry page contains geometry; a shadow page contains light-view depth.

Suppose the main camera looks at the sunlit side of the crate while the lamp illuminates its other side. Geometry invisible to the camera may still cast the crate's shadow on the wall. Reusing the camera's cluster survivor list as the complete shadow caster list would be wrong. The light's views need their own visibility and detail decisions. Conversely, a cluster that covers many camera pixels may cover very few shadow samples, so using camera detail everywhere can overspend badly.

Shadow caching exploits another kind of coherence. If a light and its occluders stay unchanged, a rendered page can remain useful across frames. If the crate moves, affected pages must be invalidated and redrawn. If every leaf moves continuously through WPO, some shadow pages may lose much of that reuse. The expense comes not only from the vertex animation itself, but from the cached work that animation makes obsolete.

The current Virtual Shadow Map cache controls include explicit deformable-mesh invalidation behavior. Nanite also has an experimental r.Nanite.VSMInvalidateOnLODDelta control initialized to zero in this checkout. Its purpose is related to geometry streaming: a shadow page rendered before desired detail arrives can otherwise retain an older geometric approximation. The control's source warns about increased invalidation when streaming struggles. This illustrates a general cache problem: newly available detail can invalidate a result even when no gameplay transform changed.

A directional sun presents a further scale issue. Nearby contact shadows need much finer spatial detail than a distant hillside. A set of spatially varying shadow regions and resolutions lets the renderer place precision near the camera and lower precision farther away. Local lights have their own projection and coverage challenges. An engineer should resist describing all of this as “Nanite draws the shadow once.” A frame may involve numerous light views, mapped page regions, cache states, and invalidation decisions.

In the courtyard, compare three changes separately. Move the camera while keeping sun, crate, and tree still; this changes demanded shadow coverage. Move the crate while keeping the camera still; this changes occluder content. Turn on wind animation for the tree; this introduces continuous deformation and potentially continuous invalidation. Capturing those cases separately reveals whether cost is driven by demand, updates, or raster work. A single average frame time over all three motions hides the reason.

Direct shadows still do not explain the warm light reflected from the crate onto nearby stone, or the bluish illumination in a region that cannot see the sun but can see the sky. Those require light arriving through directions other than a direct connection to one explicit light. Resolving that problem takes us from the geometry visibility machinery of Nanite to the transport approximations organized by Lumen.

9. Indirect illumination starts with an integral, not a light list

A surface looks bright because radiance leaves it toward the camera. Radiance describes directional light transport: both the position and the direction matter. At an opaque surface, outgoing radiance consists of emitted light plus reflected incoming light. A compact expression of that relationship is the rendering equation:

Lₒ(x, ωₒ) = Lₑ(x, ωₒ) + ∫Ω fᵣ(x, ωᵢ, ωₒ) Lᵢ(x, ωᵢ) max(0, n · ωᵢ) dωᵢ

Here x is the surface point, n its normal, ωₒ the direction toward the observer, and ωᵢ a direction from which light arrives. Lₑ is emission. The bidirectional reflectance distribution function, or BRDF, is fᵣ; it describes how the surface redistributes incoming light into the outgoing direction. The cosine factor accounts for the projected area that incoming light sees. The integral sums the contributions of directions over the relevant hemisphere.

This formula is short because it hides the world in Lᵢ. Follow an incoming direction from the stone floor and it may reach the wooden crate. The radiance leaving the crate depends on light arriving at the crate. Some of that may have come from the wall, and some from the lamp. Thus even the floor's illumination can depend on surfaces invisible to the camera and on repeated interactions. A direct-light list evaluates only selected, simpler connections within this larger problem.

For a perfectly diffuse teaching surface with reflectance ρ, the BRDF is ρ / π. The remaining cosine-weighted integral is irradiance, E, giving outgoing diffuse radiance ρE / π. This makes a useful distinction: the incoming illumination can be shared across similar nearby receivers, while each receiver still applies its own reflectance. A red wall does not merely receive “red lighting”; it changes the spectrum of the light it subsequently reflects into the room.

A brute-force numerical solution traces many paths and averages their contributions. Monte Carlo integration estimates an integral by sampling directions. For directions drawn with probability density p(ω), each sample is weighted by the integrand divided by that density. More samples reduce random error, but convergence is slow: for ordinary independent sampling, standard deviation decreases roughly with the square root of sample count. Halving noise therefore tends to demand about four times as many samples, not twice.

At 1920 by 1080, the camera has a little over two million pixels. Even one independent ray per pixel is millions of ray queries per frame, before multiple bounces, reflections, transparency, or shadows. One ray also gives a very unstable estimate of a broad directional integral. Real-time GI therefore cannot be understood only as “use a fast ray-tracing API.” It needs a strategy for choosing queries, sharing their results, limiting representation cost, and reconstructing a stable image from incomplete information.

Lumen makes several separations to enable that strategy. The representation used to intersect a ray can differ from the representation used to retrieve material and lighting at the intersection. Expensive incoming-light estimates can be stored at selected spatial locations rather than recomputed independently for every final pixel. Lighting information can persist across frames, while validity tests decide where history is reusable. Each separation introduces approximation and a possible failure mode, but also removes work that otherwise repeats.

The rest of the article will keep three questions distinct. Where does a ray hit? What radiance is available at that hit? How should sparse radiance samples be reconstructed into illumination at the camera's visible surface? Distance fields or triangle acceleration structures help with the first. Surface caches and hit lighting help with the second. Screen probes, radiance caches, integration, and temporal filtering help with the third. Lumen is the coordinated system that connects these answers; none of the individual structures is “the GI” by itself.

10. Choose what the ray is allowed to see

A ray consists of an origin, a direction, and an interval along that direction. The basic intersection question asks for the first relevant surface inside that interval. To answer it cheaply, Lumen can use information already available in the camera's image, distance-field representations of world geometry, or hardware-accelerated triangle intersections. These routes differ in what geometry they know about and how accurately they represent it.

A screen trace walks against the depth representation of the current camera image. It is attractive because visible depth and shading already exist, including fine detail and deformation represented in that image. But the screen is a limited window into the world. It contains no general information about the backs of visible objects, fully hidden surfaces, or geometry outside the view. A ray that leaves the screen or cannot establish a valid hit needs another representation. A screen-space result cannot become a complete world-space visibility solution simply by taking more steps.

This limitation explains a characteristic artifact. Put a bright emissive sign just outside the camera view but in a position that should illuminate the crate. If the sign is represented only through screen information, its contribution can change when the camera turns toward it even though the sign and crate did not move. That view dependence identifies missing or inadequate world-space representation. Disabling screen traces temporarily can reveal the underlying gap, but removing a useful first tracing stage is a diagnostic experiment rather than a universal quality improvement.

The inspected screen-probe code initializes screen traces and hierarchical depth traversal to enabled. It then compacts unresolved work for later tracing stages. In software-tracing configurations, relevant passes trace mesh signed distance fields and heightfields, followed by remaining global-field and radiance-cache work. The screen-trace controls and trace scheduling show both the layered approach and the fact that hardware and software choices are conditional. It would be inaccurate to say that every Lumen ray always traverses every representation in a fixed sequence.

A signed distance field stores a spatial function whose value approximates distance to a surface, with sign distinguishing inside from outside when the representation supports that distinction. A ray can advance by a distance estimated to be safe rather than test every triangle. The field trades exact triangle geometry for a sampled volume. Thin features, small gaps, and highly nonuniform scaling challenge that representation because finite spatial resolution can erase or distort them. Object fields retain local detail, while a global field combines information over a larger surrounding region at a different resolution and update cost.

Hardware ray tracing uses acceleration structures to skip groups of triangles efficiently. A bottom-level acceleration structure, or BLAS, organizes geometry, while a top-level acceleration structure, or TLAS, organizes instances and their transforms. Reusing one mesh in many places can reuse its geometric structure while adding instances above it. Deforming the geometry can require rebuilding or updating its lower-level representation. Hardware support accelerates traversal and intersection; it does not eliminate the cost of constructing and maintaining the scene that rays traverse.

Nanite adds a particularly important version-dependent choice. In this 5.8.1 checkout, r.RayTracing.Nanite.Mode initializes to zero: fallback mesh. Mode one selects a streamed-out mesh path. Mode two selects cluster acceleration structures, or CLAS, and falls back when the device does not support the required cluster operations. These modes and capability checks mean that neither “Lumen always traces the exact visible Nanite triangles” nor “hardware tracing can only ever use the old fallback mesh” is correct for the inspected engine.

If the camera displays fine Nanite stone chips while the selected ray-tracing representation is smoother, an indirect or reflection ray can hit a surface at a slightly different place. Screen traces may conceal some differences for visible regions, but cannot repair all off-screen cases. A useful comparison is therefore the visible mesh against the actual tracing representation in the active configuration. The correct fix depends on whether the error comes from coarse fallback geometry, field resolution, deformation support, or biased ray starts, rather than from a single generic “Lumen quality” setting.

11. A hit position is not yet a lighting answer

Suppose a ray from the floor intersects the crate. The intersection gives a position and some identity, but the GI calculation needs light leaving that position. Re-evaluating the complete material and lighting there for every ray is expensive. Lumen's Surface Cache provides reusable surface information so many queries can obtain an approximation without independently reconstructing every material and every lighting interaction.

The cache begins with parameterization: a way to map portions of a mesh surface into storage that can be sampled. Lumen uses cards, which are oriented capture regions associated with the mesh. From a card's view, the engine captures surface properties into textures. The card is not a replacement billboard that the camera renders as the object. It is an addressable view of the surface for another rendering task. Several cards are needed because one direction cannot observe every side or every concavity.

For the crate, imagine captures looking toward its front, back, top, and sides. A ray hit on the front can project into the suitable card and sample the captured surface properties. A deep recess that no selected card can see lacks good coverage even though the ray intersection itself was correct. More rays toward that recess will not create missing card coverage. This is the first reason to separate intersection quality from hit-lighting quality.

The physical textures form atlases divided into pages. A logical card can need different resolution depending on its importance and the views sampling it. Requests, mappings, and budgets decide which physical pages exist and which are refreshed. This is another virtualized representation, distinct from Nanite geometry pages and shadow pages. All three use pages because bounded demand-driven storage is useful, but the payload, validity rules, and consumers differ.

The current card capture implementation contains both mesh material shader paths and Nanite-aware capture behavior. It has separate LOD controls for static meshes and Nanite captures. This is one real technical connection between Nanite and Lumen: efficient geometry selection and multi-view rendering can make repeated material captures affordable. Lumen does not require that every surface be rendered by Nanite, and Nanite does not intrinsically calculate global illumination. They cooperate through capture work and shared scene information.

After material data is available, the cache's lighting can be updated on a different schedule. Changing the lamp's intensity should change illumination even if the crate's wood texture remains identical. Changing the wood's albedo can require material recapture and then lighting updates even if the crate's position remains fixed. The current capture controls limit pages and texels captured per frame and allocate part of the budget to refreshing existing material captures. The scene lighting controls independently govern direct-lighting and radiosity update budgets. These are multiple queues of work, not one instantaneous “rebuild GI” operation.

Radiosity is the diffuse exchange part of this cached lighting story. A surface lit by the lamp becomes a source of reflected light for other surfaces; those surfaces can in turn illuminate more surfaces. Repeatedly updating an estimate of that exchange propagates additional diffuse bounces without starting a long independent path at every camera pixel. The current Lumen radiosity implementation explicitly describes a Surface Cache gather for multibounce diffuse and exposes probe spacing, angular sampling, and filtering controls. This is separate from the final screen-probe gather: it improves the reusable lighting that later queries can find at surfaces.

Iteration and caching explain both an advantage and a limitation. A wall's computed indirect illumination can serve many camera and reflection queries, amortizing its cost. After the lamp changes, however, the surfaces that depended on its old contribution must receive fresh estimates before all later bounces agree. Filtering suppresses noise but can spread energy across insufficiently separated surfaces; limited updates preserve frame time but delay convergence. Increasing the number of rays in the final gather cannot by itself accelerate every upstream exchange that those rays sample.

Asset structure affects coverage. A single mesh containing an entire room, furniture, and recessed passages is harder to parameterize usefully than modular surfaces with understandable orientation and extent. Increasing card count may help, but it spends memory and capture work and cannot make every pathological asset equally good. The practical question is whether the important surfaces that secondary rays hit have appropriate cached data. A visually perfect main-camera mesh can still be poor input for that requirement.

Material view dependence also becomes visible here. A graph that uses the current camera direction to change base appearance may behave differently when evaluated from a card capture direction. The cached result needs an interpretation suitable for off-camera lighting queries. It is possible to provide a simpler or alternate evaluation for an auxiliary rendering context, but that is an authored approximation that should be understood. The Surface Cache is not a promise to replay arbitrary camera-dependent material behavior exactly for every future ray direction.

12. Gather light sparsely, then reconstruct it where the camera needs it

The Surface Cache can make a ray hit cheaper to shade, but millions of independently traced directional integrals are still too expensive. Lumen's screen-probe gather shares work across nearby visible surfaces. A probe is a sampling location with a compact directional description of incoming radiance. A screen probe obtains its spatial position from the camera's depth information, traces a set of directions, and stores the resulting directional samples for later filtering and integration.

The screen-space arrangement is useful because it allocates work where the camera needs a result. A coarse grid covers the image, and adaptive logic can add sampling where interpolation would otherwise fail around depth changes or small surfaces. The base downsample factor in this checkout initializes to 16: a nominal screen tile is 16 by 16 pixels before quality-dependent adjustments. This is not the assertion that every 256 pixels always share exactly one probe. The placement and interpolation controls and quality-dependent sizing show why the actual sampling layout is more nuanced.

Picture a probe located on the stone floor beside the crate. Some directions see sky, some see sunlit wall, and some see the crate's underside. That directional distribution is valuable to several nearby floor pixels. However, it should not be freely blended onto the top of the crate across a depth discontinuity. Similar image coordinates do not guarantee similar world-space illumination. Reconstruction weights therefore use geometry information to decide which samples are relevant.

Sampling direction matters as much as placing probes. Uniformly spending directions on empty or dim regions is wasteful when a small subset contributes most of the energy. Importance sampling distributes work according to information about likely lighting contributions and surface response. The estimator must still account for the sampling distribution. A biased guess that simply points every ray at the brightest previously observed region can miss newly important directions and lose energy elsewhere.

World-space radiance caching supplies another layer of sharing. Nearby screen probes may ask similar questions about distant illumination. Instead of retracing the full distant scene for each one, the system can interpolate radiance information maintained at selected world-space probe locations. Clipmaps organize these locations at multiple spatial scales around relevant views. Nearer regions can receive finer spacing, while farther regions use coarser spatial sampling. The cache stores directional lighting, not the material properties captured in surface cards.

That last distinction prevents a common conceptual collapse. A Surface Cache entry is tied to a surface parameterization: it tells us about a represented object surface. A radiance-cache probe is a sample of light in space used to help answer incoming-light queries. A screen probe is positioned according to the visible image and helps reconstruct the final gather. All are caches or probes in an everyday sense, but they differ in location, contents, update schedule, and the error they introduce.

The current screen-probe radiance-cache controls specify clipmap organization and a tracing budget. The update budget is then scaled by lighting update speed and editing state. Meanwhile, the tracing code applies radiance-cache information in the appropriate remaining trace work. These details matter because “increase GI samples” could mean increasing probe density, directional resolution, distant-cache updates, or temporal responsiveness. The chosen setting should match the missing information.

Finally, directional radiance is integrated against the receiver's response. Diffuse illumination averages a broad range of directions; rough specular illumination averages a narrower distribution; a very smooth reflection is sensitive to a much more precise direction. This difference will determine how far the screen-probe result can be reused. It also explains why a floor can have stable diffuse bounce lighting while a mirror in the same scene exposes much more obvious tracing or cache errors.

13. A mirror exposes information that diffuse lighting can average away

A diffuse wall integrates light from a broad hemisphere. If one incoming direction is slightly wrong, neighboring directions may soften the visible error. A smooth mirror is much less forgiving. Its outgoing direction corresponds to a narrow set of incoming directions, so it preserves spatial detail and reveals errors in the reflected scene. The distinction is mathematical before it becomes an engine feature: different reflectance distributions require different angular information.

A rough metal plaque in the courtyard sits between these extremes. At high roughness, its broad reflection can reuse coarser directional lighting effectively. As roughness falls, the reflection becomes sensitive to individual off-screen surfaces and highlights. Lumen therefore has dedicated reflection work and controls for which roughness range receives it. The inspected reflection implementation also includes optional radiance-cache reuse for rough reflections, with that reuse control initialized to zero. It would be inaccurate to infer that every reflection always uses the screen-probe radiance cache merely because the option exists.

Reflection intersection and reflection lighting remain separate. A hardware ray can intersect the correct triangle yet obtain an overly simplified light value from a cache. Conversely, detailed hit lighting cannot shade the correct surface if the ray intersected an inaccurate fallback mesh. This distinction is especially useful when a mirror shows the wall in the correct place but omits a sharp lamp highlight. The geometry answer may already be adequate; the lighting answer may need a richer evaluation.

In the current source, r.Lumen.HardwareRayTracing.LightingMode initializes to zero, selecting Surface Cache hit lighting. The same control documents a mode that evaluates lighting at hits for GI and reflections, and another that evaluates hit lighting for reflections while retaining cached GI behavior. The mode definitions explicitly retain the Surface Cache for secondary contributions even when hit lighting is selected. “Hit lighting” therefore does not mean that the frame has become an unbiased full path tracer.

Why does full hit evaluation cost more? A ray hit may require material shader execution, texture reads, a shading normal, direct-light selection, and shadow rays. Adjacent rays can hit different materials and unrelated memory, reducing the coherence that makes ordinary surface shading efficient. Masked geometry can require opacity tests during traversal. The current source exposes controls for hit-lighting shadows and opacity handling because these choices change both quality and cost. More accurate illumination at an intersection is a substantial rendering job, not a free switch in the intersection instruction.

Now place a glass pane in front of the plaque. The camera may need reflection from the glass, transmission through it, reflection from the plaque, and possibly another surface behind that. An opaque GBuffer stores a single frontmost surface and cannot describe this layered transport by itself. Unreal provides separate translucency-related paths and feature gates; the Lumen card shader compiler in this checkout conditionally allows translucent material captures for supported ray-traced refraction behavior. The capture eligibility logic is more precise than a blanket claim that all glass is either fully supported or absent.

For production diagnosis, begin with a simple opaque mirror, then add roughness, then add glass. If the opaque mirror already sees the wrong crate silhouette, investigate tracing geometry. If it sees the correct silhouette but inadequate highlights, compare Surface Cache lighting and the appropriate hit-lighting mode. If the problem appears only after glass is introduced, inspect the configured translucency and refraction path. Increasing a global quality scalar before separating these cases can spend a large amount of GPU time without changing the missing information.

Temporal and spatial denoising still matter even after choosing the correct tracing and lighting paths. A rough reflection samples a directional distribution, so sparse rays create noise. Reconstruction tries to reduce that noise while preserving edges and motion. The reflection source has its own temporal controls and accumulation limits. It should not be assumed to share every history policy with diffuse GI. A stable diffuse result beside a noisy reflection is compatible with both systems operating as designed under different information requirements.

14. Reusing last frame's light requires evidence that it still belongs here

Real-time rendering gains enormous efficiency from temporal coherence: most of the world changes only a little between adjacent frames. A wall that received useful GI samples a moment ago probably does not need to begin with no information now. But history is associated with surfaces and lighting states, not merely with pixel coordinates. If the camera moves, the same pixel can show a different point. If the crate moves away, a pixel that showed wood can now show stone.

Reprojection maps a current surface point toward where it appeared in the previous frame. Motion vectors are one useful carrier of that relationship. They require appropriate current and previous transforms, camera matrices, and deformation state. Keeping only the current bone pose can make an animated guard appear to have incorrect motion even if its current geometry is visually correct. The GI filter then receives misleading evidence about where history should be fetched.

Fetched history must be validated. Depth disagreement can indicate a newly exposed surface. A normal disagreement can indicate a different orientation. Lighting-change heuristics can reduce reliance on old samples around moving contributors. Neighborhood constraints can prevent a stale value from surviving far outside the range supported by current observations. Every rejection restores responsiveness but discards useful samples; every acceptance improves stability but risks carrying old light into a new situation.

The inspected screen-probe gather initializes its maximum accumulated frame control to ten, with faster-update behavior for moving-lighting situations and several rejection controls. The C++ comments and values state the tradeoff between responsiveness and noise. This does not guarantee a fixed ten-frame visual delay: histories can be rejected, update weights vary, and separate caches have their own schedules. Treat the value as part of a filter policy, not as a stopwatch for the whole GI system.

The shader side makes the retained state tangible. LumenScreenProbeGatherTemporal.usf declares histories for diffuse indirect, backface diffuse, rough specular, short-range terms, and accumulation state. This is more than a blur applied at the end. It is a set of evolving estimates whose meaning depends on correct geometry correspondence and current sampling evidence. When changing the scene representation, those histories become clients of its identity, motion, and lifetime contracts.

There are also histories before the final image filter. Surface cards may contain older material captures. Direct-lighting atlas regions may not yet have been refreshed after the lamp changes. Radiosity and world-space radiance probes may still reflect earlier illumination. Even a final filter configured for immediate response cannot invent updated input that its upstream caches have not produced. Conversely, forcing every upstream cache to update every frame can remove latency at a prohibitive cost without addressing a bad reprojection vector.

The correct experiment for a lamp toggle is therefore staged. Hold the camera and geometry still. Toggle only the light and observe both the scene-lighting representation and the final image. If the cache itself responds slowly, examine its update budget. If the cache changes quickly but the final image trails, inspect gather and reflection history. Then repeat with a moving crate to add geometric invalidation. These experiments isolate where stale information enters the chain.

A temporal design must also define discontinuities. Teleporting the camera, replacing a mesh, changing topology, or switching the rendering path can invalidate assumptions more severely than ordinary motion. Preserving history blindly makes the first few frames look smooth but wrong. Clearing everything on every small change makes them responsive but noisy. The engineering task is to describe which representation revisions invalidate which estimates, and to supply enough information for each consumer to make that decision.

15. Deformation and large worlds test every approximation at once

A moving rigid crate can reuse its mesh topology while its instance transform changes. An animated guard changes vertex positions relative to the instance. A spline-deformed pipe bends a static source mesh along a curve. A wind material displaces leaves according to time and position. These cases can all produce moving triangles on screen, but their auxiliary representations may have very different update mechanisms.

Hardware ray tracing can represent skinned geometry when the project and platform support the path, at the cost of maintaining the corresponding acceleration structures. Software distance fields generally do not become an exact animated copy simply because a vertex shader moved the mesh. A build-time field may describe the rest shape rather than the current pose. Thus support must be specified per consumer: main-view rasterization, shadow rendering, ray intersection, material capture, and temporal history can each have a different answer.

This checkout provides an important correction to older Lumen summaries. r.MeshCardRepresentation.SkeletalMesh initializes to one, and the helper permits card generation on supported Lumen platforms. The Nanite skinned proxy builds cards from its pre-skinned bounds and marks the representation for more permissive sampling because animation can disagree with the capture. See the enable and platform check and bounds-based card construction. This establishes that a skeletal card representation exists; it does not establish perfect pose-matched GI or exact software intersection.

Spline meshes show the same separation in another form. The Nanite spline proxy explicitly disables its distance-field representation and has code to construct a card representation. The spline proxy and card creation branch therefore prove two different facts: the surface can participate in one representation while being unavailable through another. In the dynamic ray-tracing path, the current Nanite source notes that spline or WPO geometry still uses fallback behavior rather than the stream-out route in that branch. The implementation restriction should qualify any claim about tracing the visible Nanite geometry.

Large worlds introduce scale and coverage instead of only deformation. A renderer cannot keep its finest fields, cards, shadow maps, and radiance probes uniformly dense across an arbitrarily large map. It concentrates detail around relevant views and manages more distant information through coarser or separate representations. As the camera moves, newly important regions need requests, captures, and lighting updates. Fast movement exposes the latency of all of these queues at once.

Far Field is a configurable hardware-tracing extension rather than an automatic property of every Lumen setup. Public documentation connects it to appropriate world-partition HLOD content. Here the exact distance default demonstrates why source versioning matters: this checkout initializes r.LumenScene.FarField to zero and r.LumenScene.FarField.MaxTraceDistance to 1.0e6 Unreal units. With the usual centimeter convention, that distance is ten kilometers, while public documentation text describes a one-kilometer default. The local source values are the baseline used here; project configuration can differ.

Extending the maximum ray distance alone does not supply all missing far geometry or lighting. The acceleration structure must include a suitable representation, culling distances must agree with the intended coverage, and hit lighting must have something meaningful to sample. A gap between near-scene coverage and far-scene coverage can produce missing occlusion even when both regions are individually configured to work. These are boundaries between representations, so diagnosing them requires visualizing both sides of the boundary.

The courtyard makes a useful small-scale version of the problem. First walk slowly around the tree, then teleport to the opposite gate. The slow walk lets caches evolve with demand. The teleport asks every subsystem to discover a new visible set at once. If Nanite detail refines first, shadow pages update next, and indirect illumination settles afterward, the sequence is understandable from their different representations and budgets. It should be measured and designed, not hidden behind the claim that the scene is simply either loaded or unloaded.

16. Follow one changed frame through the courtyard

We can now follow a concrete frame without treating any subsystem as a black box. At the start of this example, the camera advances toward the gate, the crate slides half a meter toward the wall, the guard raises an arm, the leaves sway, and the lamp changes from dim orange to bright white. The visible frame must combine these changes with work retained from earlier frames. The exact scheduling can overlap through the render graph, but the data dependencies remain understandable.

The scene update first communicates changed instance transforms, deformation inputs, material parameters, and light parameters to rendering state. The crate's reusable geometry asset did not change, but its current and previous placement differ. The guard needs current and previous deformation information appropriate to its rendering path. The lamp's light record changes without forcing the wall's material graph to be recompiled. Stable identities allow caches and GPU tables to recognize that these are updates to existing objects rather than unrelated replacements.

For the main view, Nanite traverses relevant instances and bounds. The approaching camera demands finer wall detail, so traversal may emit streaming requests for pages that were unnecessary at the gate. This frame uses the available valid representation. Previous occlusion helps reject work in the main pass, while a post pass can recover parts of the crate or wall newly revealed by motion. The moving leaves require appropriate bounds and programmable raster work for their deformation and coverage.

Rasterization establishes visible surface identities and depths. Material evaluation retrieves the winning wall, crate, foliage, and other surface data and produces the opaque shading inputs. Conventional geometry can contribute through its own supported path to compatible scene textures. The lamp's bright new direct illumination can be evaluated from current light state. Shadow views must account for the crate's new position and the relevant leaf motion; cached pages affected by those changes cannot simply be reused as though the scene were static.

In parallel with the camera's needs, Lumen maintains its world representations. Rigid movement changes instance placement and the regions affected by the crate. Deformation may require ray-tracing geometry updates and uses whatever card approximation is supported. The lamp's color change changes lighting in the cache even if every material capture remains valid. If the leaf material's appearance changes over time, its captured properties may also need refresh. These are separate causes of work, so a renderer should not represent them as one undifferentiated dirty bit.

The floor's screen probes trace incoming directions. Some find reliable screen-space hits on the now-visible crate or wall. Others leave the image and need the configured world representation. A hardware ray may see fallback or other supported Nanite ray-tracing geometry; a software ray may rely on a field that omits a thin or deforming object. At a valid hit, the query retrieves cached lighting or takes an appropriate hit-lighting route. Distant radiance-cache data can help complete the incoming-light estimate.

Probe filtering and integration reconstruct diffuse and rough lighting for the visible surfaces. The metal plaque asks for more precise reflection information and may receive dedicated reflection rays. The glass pane follows its configured translucent rendering and lighting path. The result still needs temporal processing: pixels that continue tracking the crate can reuse matching history, whereas newly revealed wall pixels must reject crate history. The lamp change should reduce trust in older illumination even where geometry correspondence remains valid.

This frame can therefore contain several kinds of legitimate approximation simultaneously. The wall's geometry may be temporarily coarser while pages arrive. The guard's card data may approximate its animated surface. The plaque's reflection may use a different geometry representation from the camera. Some indirect light may still blend with earlier estimates. The correct quality question is whether these approximations remain bounded and visually acceptable in the intended motion, not whether every internal representation is byte-for-byte identical.

Now make the example deliberately wrong. Keep the crate's old bounds after moving it, fail to supply previous deformation for the guard, leave a stale light value in the cache, and ray trace a coarse fallback under a highly detailed mirror. The resulting missing geometry, motion trails, lighting lag, and reflection mismatch have different causes even though they all occur in one frame. A modular renderer should expose enough intermediate state to distinguish them quickly. Otherwise, every bug appears to be “Nanite flickers” or “Lumen is wrong,” and the architecture becomes difficult to improve.

17. Diagnose the representation that lost the needed information

Debugging is most effective when it begins with a hypothesis about missing information. A hole in the main-view silhouette points toward geometry selection, bounds, residency, or coverage. Correct silhouettes with incorrect textures point toward material evaluation or attribute reconstruction. Correct primary rendering with wrong reflections points toward ray geometry, hit lighting, or reflection reconstruction. A delayed response after an otherwise correct change points toward invalidation and update schedules. These distinctions narrow the investigation before a single quality setting is changed.

If the courtyard wall refines visibly after rapid motion, inspect streaming demand and residency before changing material complexity. If its geometry vanishes when leaves bend, inspect deformation bounds and culling. If dense foliage is slow despite modest visible area, compare surviving geometry, layered coverage, and programmable raster work. A triangle view alone cannot reveal the cost of opacity evaluation, and a material-bin view alone cannot reveal how many competing surfaces each bin rasterizes.

For Lumen, compare the ordinary scene with the representations that its queries use. Geometry normals can expose a mismatch in the traced shape. Mesh and global distance-field visualizations show whether software tracing has an adequate spatial approximation. Surface Cache visualization exposes parameterization coverage. A reflection view reveals the scene from that consumer's limits. The purpose is to identify the first stage where the expected information disappears.

Suppose a narrow doorway leaks light into the shaded courtyard. First inspect the tracing representation. A coarse distance field may erase the wall thickness or opening boundary. Increasing final-gather samples would repeatedly query the same inadequate geometry. If intersection is correct but the doorway's surfaces are pink in Surface Cache view, change card coverage or asset decomposition instead. If both representations are adequate but the leak appears only during camera motion, examine screen-trace transitions and temporal reconstruction.

Suppose the orange lamp switches off but warm light remains on the wall. Inspect lighting-cache updates with the camera still. If the cached lighting fades slowly, increasing only final-image history responsiveness cannot solve the source lag. If the cache responds promptly, a temporal filter may be retaining older estimates. The current source offers controls for card capture, direct lighting, radiosity, radiance probes, diffuse history, and reflection history precisely because the costs and latencies belong to separate pieces.

Measure on the target configuration. Record resolution, platform, RHI, scalability, ray-tracing mode, material settings, asset state, and whether caches are warm. Use GPU timings to separate culling, rasterization, material shading, shadow work, acceleration-structure updates, Lumen scene updates, tracing, and reconstruction where the profiler exposes them. A change that lowers the main Nanite pass while raising shadow invalidation can lose overall. A larger cache that improves a stationary view may still struggle after a teleport if update bandwidth is unchanged.

Useful experiments change one causal factor at a time. Freeze wind to reveal deformation and invalidation cost. Keep geometry static while moving only the camera to reveal visibility and demand changes. Hold the camera still while moving only the lamp to reveal illumination latency. Compare software and hardware tracing only after confirming both configurations actually represent the test objects. Such controlled comparisons provide more actionable evidence than a collection of arbitrary console-variable values copied from unrelated scenes.

A performance conclusion should distinguish an observed measurement from a prediction. The examples in this article predict mechanisms and likely bottlenecks; they do not claim measured milliseconds for Arda or the local Unreal build. Before adopting a setting, collect a repeatable camera path and a representative dynamic workload. The renderer's job is to maintain quality throughout that workload, including transitions, rather than to optimize one settled screenshot.

18. What this teaches us about building the corresponding systems in Arda

The most useful architectural lesson is not to reproduce Unreal's class list. It is to recognize why one logical object needs several derived representations. The crate has authoring geometry, runtime raster geometry, instance placement, material bindings, shadow participation, ray-tracing geometry or distance-field participation, surface-cache coverage, and temporal identity. These representations share meaning but have different storage, update costs, and validity periods. Forcing them into one universal mesh object makes every new rendering feature modify every other feature's assumptions.

Arda should therefore make the logical surface and its instances stable concepts, while allowing rasterization, tracing, and lighting caches to maintain specialized data. A geometry resource can own immutable source-derived information and publish capability information. An instance can own placement, material overrides, and revisions. A raster backend can choose conventional draws or a virtualized cluster representation. A tracing backend can provide an intersection representation with an explicit approximation policy. A lighting-cache backend can parameterize and refresh surface information without pretending to be the geometry itself.

Those responsibilities require more than interfaces named after features. They require precise update messages. A transform revision changes placement and affected spatial regions. A deformation revision changes current shape and motion correspondence. A material revision can change shading, coverage, displacement bounds, or emitted light; those consequences are not interchangeable. A lighting revision can invalidate cached illumination without changing material captures. A residency revision can expose finer geometry and make cached shadows or intersections stale. Each consumer should subscribe to the changes that affect its answer.

Stable handles need lifetime rules. If the crate is removed and a new lamp receives the same numeric slot, an old cache entry must not silently become associated with the lamp. A generation attached to the handle can distinguish the new occupant, while retirement rules prevent GPU work still in flight from reading reclaimed memory. Frame-local indices can be compact for performance, but they should be derived from stable identity with explicit remapping. The independent scene representation article develops the CPU/GPU ownership side of this design.

The first implementation does not need Nanite's entire feature set. A conventional mesh backend with correct transforms, materials, motion, and bounds can establish the scene contract. A simple cluster culler can later consume the same instance identity. Add local LOD only after compatible replacement and error metadata are testable. Add streaming only after a valid resident representation remains drawable under constrained memory. This order makes each optimization preserve an already understood answer rather than introducing several forms of incompleteness at once.

Global illumination should follow the same discipline. Begin with an intersection abstraction that reports which representation produced the hit and what confidence or approximation applies. Add a surface lookup contract with explicit missing-coverage behavior. Then add sparse sampling and history with visible debug outputs. A cache miss, a trace miss, an unrepresented object, and a temporally rejected sample should not all become the same black pixel without explanation. The renderer needs observable reasons for missing information if it is to be engineered rather than tuned by superstition.

Budgets belong in the architecture from the beginning. Geometry pages, shadow pages, card captures, acceleration-structure updates, and radiance probes compete for memory and frame time. Their work queues should expose demand, completed work, age, and failure or fallback behavior. A budget limits how much work happens now; a policy decides which work matters most. Keeping those concepts separate makes it possible to prioritize newly visible geometry or important lighting changes without rewriting the storage mechanism.

The courtyard can become an acceptance scene for these contracts. A solid high-detail wall tests local detail and streaming. A moving crate tests rigid updates and shadow invalidation. A guard tests deformation and temporal correspondence. A tree tests bounds, masked coverage, and layered visibility. A lamp toggle tests lighting-cache freshness. A mirror and glass pane test whether different visibility and transport paths agree well enough. Each object exists to reveal one dependency that a beautiful static scene might conceal.

The final engineering question is whether a subsystem can state what answer it provides, what information it omits, and when that answer becomes invalid. Nanite answers primary and auxiliary raster visibility efficiently using compatible geometric approximations and demand-driven residency. Lumen combines several intersection, surface-lighting, sampling, and reconstruction representations to estimate changing illumination. Their effectiveness comes from those explicit approximations and the mechanisms that coordinate them. That is the foundation worth carrying into Arda: representations with clear meanings, bounded costs, and inspectable transitions as the world changes.
