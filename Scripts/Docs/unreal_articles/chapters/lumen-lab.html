Stand in the doorway of a room with a red wall, a green wall, two boxes, and one ceiling light. The ceiling receives almost no direct light from a downward-facing emitter, yet it is visible. The dark side of a box receives color from nearby walls. A glossy object reflects surfaces outside the camera's image. These three observations already specify the problem: a renderer must estimate light arriving from other surfaces, including surfaces it cannot currently see.

This essay builds that estimate in a small, reproducible renderer and then maps each representation to Unreal Engine's Lumen implementation. The laboratory is actual computation: the room is intersected by rays, material properties are captured into arrays, a sampled transport operator propagates diffuse light, probes record directional radiance, and reflection rays evaluate a rough-surface model. The figures labeled computed CPU reference come from that code. They are a reduced model of the algorithms, not captures of Unreal's internal buffers. Genuine Unreal captures elsewhere in this essay are identified separately. The distinction matters because matching a useful idea is easier than matching Lumen's complete production implementation.

The source baseline is Unreal Engine 5.8.1 at 71fe36aac5a8df5ccd66c763ffc902b29b6a9c43. The implementation we develop is an intentionally inspectable route toward a Lumen-like renderer: material caches, several visibility representations, sparse directional sampling, reconstruction, and temporal reuse. It does not claim parity with all of Unreal's shader permutations, platform paths, material models, large-world streaming, or quality policies. Mathematical derivations state their assumptions so that you can distinguish a correct estimator from an approximation added to fit a frame budget.

Start with a room whose answers we can inspect

The room is four meters wide, five meters deep, and three meters high. The front is open to a black environment. Its red wall lies at \(x=-2\); its green wall lies at \(x=2\). The floor is \(z=0\), the ceiling is \(z=3\), and the back wall is \(y=5\). A 1.3 by 1.2 meter emitter sits just below the ceiling and emits downward. One short matte box occupies the left side; one tall box on the right supplies our glossy reflection example. Separate surfaces make visibility and cache ownership unambiguous.

The camera starts at (0, -4, 1.65), looks toward (0, 2.8, 1.4), and uses a 43-degree vertical field of view. The reference image contains 560 by 400 pixels. Every primary ray chooses the nearest valid intersection with the room's rectangles. Its hit point, normal, and material identify a visible surface. This is our equivalent of the information a deferred renderer makes available through depth and its GBuffer; the reference uses ray intersections to keep the geometry code short.

A GBuffer records the surface visible from this camera. That is not enough to shade indirect light. Imagine a ray leaving the back of the short box and hitting the red wall. Its destination may have no visible pixel in the camera image. We therefore need a second, camera-independent way to find material and lighting at that destination. This is the reason for a surface cache, not merely an optimization of the camera's GBuffer.

We also need to separate two questions that are often conflated. A visibility representation answers where a ray stops. A lighting representation answers what radiance arrives from there. Triangle intersections, distance fields, and screen depth can answer the first question with different coverage and precision. A material evaluation, a surface-lighting texel, or a directional probe can help answer the second. A distance-field hit has no inherent color; a bright cached texel does not prove that a receiver can see it.

All computed results use a black environment, fixed geometry, Lambertian diffuse transport, and one rectangular emitter. The reflection experiment adds a microfacet model only for the camera-visible tall box. This deliberately leaves indirect glossy paths out of the diffuse transport solve. It lets us observe the major representations without hiding errors behind a complex scene. The downloadable scene description records every face, bound, normal, and material; the computed arrays retain linear values before display conversion.

Turn “light bouncing around” into a solvable equation

Let \(x\) be a surface point, \(n\) its outward unit normal, and \(\omega_o\) a unit direction toward an observer. Radiance \(L_o(x,\omega_o)\) is the light leaving that point in that direction. Radiance has units of power per projected area per solid angle. It is directional: a mirror can be bright from one direction and dark from another even though the surface point is unchanged.

For an opaque reflecting surface, split outgoing radiance into self-emission and reflected incoming light. The BRDF \(f_r\) states how strongly a small incoming directional contribution is scattered toward the observer. Projecting that incoming direction onto the surface contributes the cosine factor:

\[ L_o(x,\omega_o)=L_e(x,\omega_o)+ \int_{\Omega^+(n)} f_r(x,\omega_i,\omega_o) L_i(x,\omega_i)(n\cdot\omega_i)\,d\omega_i. \]

Here \(\Omega^+(n)\) is the outward hemisphere. Incoming radiance is itself outgoing radiance from the first visible surface \(y\) along the ray: \(L_i(x,\omega_i)=L_o(y,-\omega_i)\). A ray escaping our room returns zero. Thus the equation is recursive across surfaces. The same relation explains a second bounce, a tenth bounce, and the color bleeding in the room; no separate “color bleed effect” is needed.

For a Lambertian surface, \(f_r=\rho/\pi\), where RGB reflectance \(\rho\) lies between zero and one. Why divide by pi? A constant incoming radiance must not gain energy when reflected by a white diffuse surface. In spherical coordinates, \(d\omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\), so:

\[ \int_{\Omega^+}\cos\theta\,d\omega =\int_0^{2\pi}\!d\phi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta =2\pi\left[\frac{\sin^2\theta}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/2}=\pi. \]

The factor \(\rho/\pi\) therefore returns exactly the fraction \(\rho\) of a constant illumination field. Define irradiance \(E(x)=\int L_i\cos\theta\,d\omega\). Diffuse outgoing radiance is \(\rho E/\pi\). A probe that stores irradiance cannot be substituted for a probe that stores directional radiance; their units and consumers differ.

The GPU cannot recursively solve the full equation at every visible point within an ordinary frame budget. Our first reduction is to discretize diffuse outgoing radiance over surfaces. Our second is to sample directions instead of integrating every direction. Our third is to reuse estimates across locations and frames. Each reduction introduces a specific error: finite spatial resolution, sampling noise, interpolation bias, or stale history. Keeping those error sources distinct makes debugging tractable.

This equation also clarifies a boundary with the main renderer. Lumen contributes estimates of indirect diffuse lighting and reflected radiance to the broader shading pipeline. It does not replace the existence of surface materials, camera visibility, all direct-light evaluation, or the final display transform. In our lab, direct lighting is deliberately computed separately so that a second bounce can be inspected without double-counting the light source.

Give each surface a stable place to store material and lighting

Subdivide the room's planar faces into texels with a target spacing of 23 centimeters. There are 1,985 texels including the emitter. Each texel stores a surface point, a normal, diffuse reflectance, and a face identity. The face's local two-dimensional coordinates map a hit point to the neighboring texels. This is an exact, simple parameterization for rectangles. It is our educational equivalent of an orthographic surface card.

A card is not a replacement mesh. It is a capture direction, a footprint, and a mapping between surface positions and an image. For our planar faces, put an orthographic camera one meter along the normal, looking back at the face. Every covered texel has capture depth one meter. Captured normal and material are constant per face, while captured lighting varies across it. The atlas below therefore contains flat depth islands for a geometric reason; no meaningful depth variation was omitted.

Real meshes require several capture directions, coverage tests, depth, and a policy for choosing among cards. A concavity may be invisible to every selected capture. A large mesh containing the entire room can be harder to cover than separate walls and objects. Trace a ray through empty space without testing every triangle

For our rectangles, exact intersection is cheap: solve the ray's plane equation, test its two in-plane bounds, and choose the smallest positive distance. We retain that implementation as a reference. A more general scene benefits from an acceleration structure or a distance representation. A signed distance field gives positive distance outside a solid, zero on its boundary, and negative distance inside. It describes geometry, not illumination.

For an axis-aligned box centered at \(c\) with half-extents \(b\), first calculate componentwise excess beyond its bounds: \(q=|x-c|-b\). Positive components identify directions in which the point lies outside. Their Euclidean length is the distance to an outside face, edge, or corner. If every component is nonpositive, the largest component is the signed distance to the nearest inside face:

\[ d_{\rm box}(x)=\|\max(q,0)\|_2+ \min\bigl(\max(q_x,q_y,q_z),0\bigr). \]

The lab models walls as ten-centimeter-thick boxes and uses the minimum distance over the wall and object solids. Outside their union, this minimum is the distance to the nearest solid. The emitter is intentionally absent from this SDF diagnostic; the tested rays stay in a horizontal plane below it. We compare like-for-like visibility against the exact rectangles at that height, rather than claiming an all-scene comparison with mismatched geometry.

The safe stepping rule follows from a geometric bound. If a field \(f\) is Lipschitz with constant \(K\), then \(|f(x)-f(y)|\le K\|x-y\|\). At any zero-surface point \(y\), this becomes \(|f(x)|\le K\|x-y\|\). Therefore no zero can be closer than \(|f(x)|/K\). For a normalized ray direction and an exact outside distance field, \(K=1\):

\[ r(t)=o+t\omega,\quad \|\omega\|=1, \qquad t_{k+1}=t_k+d(r(t_k)). \]

Each step crosses a sphere known to contain no surface in its interior. Stop when distance falls below a tolerance, the ray leaves the tracing domain, or a maximum iteration budget is exhausted. An exhausted budget is an unresolved trace, not evidence of a miss. Grazing rays converge slowly because distance to the surface can be tiny while remaining distance along the ray is large. This argument is the foundation of Hart's sphere tracing method.

A regression makes that distinction concrete: a ray starting at (0, 0.25, 0.0001) meters and traveling along +Y stays only 0.1 mm above the floor. After 256 safe advances it has traveled roughly 0.0255 m, although exact intersection places the back-wall hit at 4.75 m. The marcher reports budget_exhausted and an undefined hit distance, not a miss. A production consumer must retry, use a fallback, or carry uncertainty rather than replace this unresolved trace with sky lighting. The 256 ordinary horizontal validation rays all resolve; this additional grazing ray deliberately does not.

Unreal uses sampled mesh distance fields and a coarser global representation, not our analytic box function. Sampling, quantization, mip selection, nonuniform scale, and finite trace footprints complicate the bound. If an interpolated sampled field has a known absolute error bound \(\epsilon_d\), a conservative outside advance is \(\max(0,\tilde d-\epsilon_d)/K\). A practical positive minimum step can improve progress, but it gives up the strict no-crossing guarantee unless accompanied by a suitable intersection strategy. Thin geometry is especially vulnerable when representation resolution exceeds wall thickness.

The current screen-probe shaders have separate mesh-SDF tracing and voxel/global-scene tracing paths. The latter can limit a trace when valid radiance-cache coverage is reached; it constructs a cone-trace input and requests scene-voxel tracing under the corresponding shader permutation. Thus “trace the SDF” is one stage in a larger dispatch and fallback policy. Mesh-SDF path; radiance-cache coverage and voxel tracing.

The lab's 256 horizontal test rays all agree with exact geometry on hit versus miss. Their largest hit-distance difference is approximately 0.121 millimeters. This validates the analytic marcher and tolerance for those rays. It does not validate a voxelized field, thin imported meshes, or every direction. Those are separate tests an implementation must add when it replaces the analytic field with actual mesh data.

Derive the estimator before choosing a sample pattern

A ray gives one directional observation; lighting is an integral over directions. Let \(g(\omega)=f_rL_i\cos\theta\) and choose a normalized probability density \(p(\omega)\) with respect to solid angle. It must be positive wherever \(g\) can be nonzero. Insert \(p/p\) into the integral and interpret it as an expectation:

\[ I=\int g(\omega)\,d\omega =\int \frac{g(\omega)}{p(\omega)}p(\omega)\,d\omega =\mathbb E_p\!\left[\frac{g(\omega)}{p(\omega)}\right], \qquad \widehat I_N=\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N\frac{g(\omega_j)}{p(\omega_j)}. \]

Linearity of expectation gives \(\mathbb E[\widehat I_N]=I\). For independent identically distributed samples with finite second moment, cross-covariances vanish and:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\widehat I_N) =\frac1N\left[\int\frac{g(\omega)^2}{p(\omega)}\,d\omega-I^2\right]. \]

This explains both the familiar inverse-square-root reduction in noise and the value of importance sampling: place probability where the integrand is large. It also explains why “trace more bright directions” is incomplete. The contribution must be divided by the density that generated it. A density in texture-coordinate space cannot be used as though it were a density in solid angle; the coordinate transformation's Jacobian must be included. The general probability and estimator framework is developed in PBRT's Monte Carlo chapter; the derivation above specializes it to our hemisphere integral.

For diffuse reflection, choose \(p(\omega)=\cos\theta/\pi\). The normalization follows from the cosine integral we already evaluated. Multiplying by the spherical-coordinate Jacobian gives joint coordinate density \(p(\theta,\phi)=\cos\theta\sin\theta/\pi\). Its azimuthal marginal is uniform, while its polar marginal is \(p_\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\). Integrate the latter to obtain the cumulative distribution:

\[ F_\theta(\theta)=\int_0^\theta 2\sin t\cos t\,dt=\sin^2\theta. \quad u_1=\sin^2\theta\Rightarrow \sin\theta=\sqrt{u_1},\quad \cos\theta=\sqrt{1-u_1}; \quad \phi=2\pi u_2. \]

For independent uniform \(u_1,u_2\in[0,1)\), construct \((\sqrt{u_1}\cos\phi,\sqrt{u_1}\sin\phi,\sqrt{1-u_1})\). Rotate this direction through an orthonormal tangent, bitangent, and normal basis at the receiver. The basis preserves solid angle. For Lambertian reflection, the BRDF and cosine then cancel the PDF exactly:

\[ \widehat L_{\rm diffuse} =\frac1N\sum_j\frac{(\rho/\pi)L_i(\omega_j)\cos\theta_j}{\cos\theta_j/\pi} =\frac{\rho}{N}\sum_j L_i(\omega_j). \]

This is why the reference code can average incoming radiance and multiply by albedo. That shortcut is valid because of a particular sampling distribution and BRDF, not because diffuse lighting is always a plain average. With uniform hemisphere sampling, \(p=1/(2\pi)\), each sample instead contributes \(2\rho L_i\cos\theta\).

The constant-light variance follows directly: uniform-hemisphere \(z=\cos\theta\) is uniform on \([0,1]\), so the sample contribution is \(2z\), its expectation is one, and its second moment is \(4/3\). The variance is \(4/3-1=1/3\). A sampler that fails this test should not be debugged inside a moving, denoised game scene.

Lumen can also guide samples using previous lighting estimates. In the inspected shader, luminance supplies a lighting PDF which is normalized over directional texels. That is useful guidance, but old lighting is not truth: an emitter can turn on where the old density was tiny. A robust general mixture includes a component with support everywhere needed, such as \(p=\beta p_{\rm cosine}+(1-\beta)p_{\rm guide}\) with \(0<\beta\le1\). Select a component with its mixture probability, then divide by the full mixture density, not merely the selected component's density. This mixture is an implementation recommendation for our renderer, not a claim that every Lumen permutation uses this exact formula. Lumen's luminance-guided directional PDF.

Illuminate the captured surfaces before asking them to bounce light

Start with the ceiling emitter. Sampling arbitrary hemisphere directions wastes most rays because the light occupies only a small solid angle from many receivers. Instead, sample a point \(y\) uniformly on its area \(A\). The area PDF is \(p_A=1/A\). Let \(r=\|y-x\|\) and \(\omega=(y-x)/r\). A small emitter area projects to solid angle \(d\omega=\cos\theta_y\,dA/r^2\), where \(\cos\theta_y=\max(0,n_y\cdot(-\omega))\). Substituting this into the rendering equation gives:

\[ L_{\rm direct}(x)=\frac{\rho(x)}\pi \int_A L_e(y,-\omega)\,V(x,y) \frac{\cos\theta_x\cos\theta_y}{r^2}\,dA, \] \[ \widehat L_{\rm direct}(x)=\frac{\rho(x)A}{\pi N} \sum_{j=1}^N L_e(y_j,-\omega_j)V(x,y_j) \frac{\cos\theta_{x,j}\cos\theta_{y,j}}{r_j^2}. \]

Visibility \(V\) is one if no surface blocks the segment and zero otherwise. Both cosines matter. The receiver cosine accounts for projected receiving area; the emitter cosine accounts for the light's projected area. The inverse-square factor emerges from conversion between area and solid angle. A frequent bug is to divide by squared distance again after using a light PDF already converted to solid angle, producing incorrect falloff.

The laboratory integrates the area with an 8 by 8 grid of cell centers and exact shadow rays. This is deterministic midpoint quadrature, not independent Monte Carlo. The preceding estimator is unbiased for genuinely random uniform area samples; the implemented midpoint version is a finite-resolution approximation, particularly near discontinuous shadow edges. We retain 64 samples per card texel so that the direct-light input is stable while studying the noisier diffuse propagation. Changing this to random stratified positions is a small experiment, but its uncertainty should then be measured separately.

The direct-light-only image has a dark ceiling and dark box fronts. That is expected: the downward emitter cannot directly light the ceiling, and the boxes occlude some emitter directions. Direct shadowing is a geometric visibility result. Making these surfaces bright with a uniform ambient term would conceal the very indirect paths we want to implement. They become visible in the next stage because other illuminated surfaces send radiance toward them.

Maintain the convention that the direct buffer contains outgoing diffuse radiance after the receiver's albedo and pi factor. The emitter texels contain emitted radiance instead. A different implementation may store irradiance and multiply by material later, but it must use that convention consistently. If one pass writes irradiance while another assumes outgoing radiance, white surfaces may seem plausible while colored walls reveal severe energy errors.

The full engine integrates surface lighting through dedicated direct-light and radiosity systems with tile selection, light data, shadowing, temporal statistics, and update budgets. The reference's single rectangular emitter isolates the same dependency: captured material and visibility feed surface lighting. It does not reproduce all Unreal light types or its stochastic direct-light implementation. The concrete handoff remains visible in the distinct direct and indirect lighting atlas resources described earlier.

Make the room light itself through a sampled transport operator

Now send 512 cosine-distributed rays from each card texel. Each ray either escapes, hits the emitter, or hits another surface texel. Escapes contribute zero. Emitter hits are excluded from this indirect operator because direct emitter illumination was already evaluated by area sampling. Every other hit records which texel's outgoing radiance will be read. This produces a sparse matrix \(K\): entry \(K_{ij}\) is the fraction of rays from receiver \(i\) that hit source texel \(j\).

Because these rays were cosine distributed, the diffuse estimator is simply reflectance times the average incoming radiance. Let \(R\) be the diagonal matrix of one color channel's reflectances, \(D\) the already computed emitted-plus-direct outgoing radiance, and \(L\) the total outgoing radiance at every texel. Then:

\[ L=D+RKL,\qquad L^{(0)}=D,\qquad L^{(k+1)}=D+RKL^{(k)}. \]

Expand the first iterations. The first update gives \(D+RKD\): direct light plus one diffuse reflection of direct light. The next adds \((RK)^2D\). After \(k\) updates, the sum is \(\sum_{b=0}^k(RK)^bD\). This is the operational meaning of a bounce in this experiment. A cache update iteration is not generally identical to one additional full-scene bounce in a production renderer, because only some cache locations may update each frame.

Why does this iteration converge here? Every row sum of \(K\) is at most one, because each sample chooses at most one destination. The largest reflectance in the scene is 0.72. Thus \(\|RK\|_\infty\le0.72<1\). For two candidate solutions \(a,b\), the update makes their maximum difference at most 0.72 times its previous value. A unique fixed point exists for this finite matrix, and the Neumann series converges:

\[ L^*=(I-RK)^{-1}D=\sum_{b=0}^{\infty}(RK)^bD, \qquad \|L^{(k)}-L^*\|_\infty\le0.72^k\|D-L^*\|_\infty. \]

This proof applies to the sampled, discretized operator, not to an exact continuum solution. Holding the random rays fixed makes iteration convergence easy to measure but leaves sampling error in \(K\). Reusing that same random matrix through a nonlinear inverse also means the final fixed point is not automatically an unbiased estimator of the continuum solution. More cache iterations reduce solve residual; more surface resolution and visibility samples reduce different errors. These are independent quality controls.

Distinguish an iteration update from the residual at the resulting iterate. Write the update map as \(F(L)=D+RKL\), the contraction bound as \(q=0.72\), and the measured change as \(\Delta_k=\|L^{(k)}-L^{(k-1)}\|_\infty\). The residual at the new iterate obeys \(r_k=\|F(L^{(k)})-L^{(k)}\|_\infty\le q\Delta_k\). Apply the triangle inequality to the unknown fixed point \(L^*=F(L^*)\):

\[\|L^*-L^{(k)}\|_\infty\le q\|L^*-L^{(k)}\|_\infty+r_k \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \|L^*-L^{(k)}\|_\infty\le\frac{r_k}{1-q}\le\frac{q\Delta_k}{1-q}.\]

With the recorded final update, the error to this particular discrete fixed point is therefore bounded by approximately \(8.70\times10^{-7}\) in the lab's radiance units. The plot reports \(\Delta_k\), not an independently measured continuum error.

The run produces 490,891 nonzero matrix entries. The maximum per-channel update falls from about 0.2305 after the first iteration to \(3.38\times10^{-7}\) after 24. All cached radiance remains finite and nonnegative. These measurements are useful engineering checks: a growing residual can indicate an energy gain, a mistaken PDF, or double-counted emitter contributions. A tiny residual cannot certify correct geometry or remove a biased visibility approximation.

Unreal's radiosity implementation explicitly describes its job as gathering indirect illumination from the Surface Cache to provide multibounce diffuse. It also exposes probe spacing, hemisphere resolution, and spatial filtering controls; its source notes the tradeoff between reduced noise and increased leaking. That is the production counterpart of our operator's sampling and reconstruction decisions. Record directional light in empty space

A surface cache stores information attached to surfaces. A world probe stores information at a position in space. Place a probe near the middle of the room, trace directions around the sphere, and fetch cached outgoing radiance at each hit. The resulting directional function says what light arrives at that probe from every direction. It can be reused by nearby queries, especially when exact long-distance tracing is too expensive.

Our room contains 69 probes after candidate positions inside solids or too close to surfaces are rejected. Each probe traces a 64 by 32 latitude-longitude set of directions. This layout is deliberately easy to plot. Equal-sized latitude-longitude texels do not cover equal solid angles: texels near a pole subtend less of the sphere. For a cell spanning azimuth \([\phi_0,\phi_1]\) and polar angle \([\theta_0,\theta_1]\), integrate the spherical Jacobian exactly:

\[ \Delta\omega =\int_{\phi_0}^{\phi_1}\int_{\theta_0}^{\theta_1}\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi =(\phi_1-\phi_0)(\cos\theta_0-\cos\theta_1). \]

The cell weights sum to \(4\pi\). To estimate irradiance for a receiving normal \(n\), use directional radiance samples \(L_j\) and those solid angles:

\[ E(n)\approx\sum_j L_j\max(0,n\cdot\omega_j)\Delta\omega_j. \]

This is quadrature over a sampled directional field. The numerical integration remains approximate because radiance and cosine vary inside each cell. The exact sum of weights verifies coordinate handling; it does not make low-resolution lighting integration exact. A bright small emitter may occupy only a few samples. The reference's plots expose that finite angular resolution instead of silently treating a directional texture as uniformly weighted pixels.

To answer a query between probes, interpolation is necessary. On a regular grid, trilinear weights follow from the query's fractional cell coordinates: for corner bits \(a,b,c\in\{0,1\}\), multiply the appropriate factors \(u\) or \(1-u\), and likewise for \(v,w\). The eight weights sum to one. A general normalized blend is \(\widehat L(x,\omega)=\sum_j w_jL_j(\omega)/\sum_jw_j\). Normalization preserves a constant field, but it does not guarantee visibility correctness.

Our probe positions are filtered around solids, so the demonstration uses eight nearest probes with inverse-square distance weights rather than pretending the filtered set remains a complete regular grid. The comparison additionally gates each contribution by a real visibility ray from the floor query to the probe. A probe behind a box must not illuminate its shadowed side merely because it is nearby. Visibility gating reduces that mistake; it cannot reconstruct missing spatial detail from a coarse set of samples.

Unreal's radiance cache is budgeted and demand driven. Its update interface states that it marks positions needing coverage, places or reuses probes, and traces a subset before exposing interpolation parameters. Clipmap cell sizes organize coverage at multiple spatial scales. Indirection maps world cells to physical atlas storage; a probe's place in the world should not be confused with a permanent atlas address. UpdateRadianceCaches.

Spend near-field rays where the camera needs them

World probes are reusable but relatively coarse. The camera needs more detail around visible geometry, especially near corners, narrow gaps, and silhouettes. A screen probe starts from a visible surface point reconstructed from depth. It records incoming directional radiance around that point. The screen selects where probes are placed; the rays themselves travel in world space.

The reference places a probe every 12 pixels where geometry is visible, yielding 1,314 probes. It traces 256 cosine directions from each. Emitter hits are removed from these probe contributions because our final composite adds the separately computed direct-light buffer. The average therefore stores indirect irradiance divided by pi. For a nearby visible pixel, the final diffuse contribution is its own material reflectance times an interpolated probe value. Do not multiply by the probe surface's albedo again: the probe represents incoming light, not the outgoing color of its placement surface.

Four nearby probes are considered for each pixel. We weight them by world-space proximity and normal agreement. The normal term is \(\max(0,n_x\cdot n_j)^{16}\); the distance term is \(\exp(-6\|x-x_j\|)\) with distances measured in meters. Normalize the product weights before blending. These constants are explicit choices for this small room, not Unreal defaults. This limited gather illustrates why naive bilinear interpolation in screen coordinates would mix a box front with the wall behind it.

The measured mean absolute linear-channel difference is approximately 0.00467 over visible geometry. That scalar hides spatially concentrated errors, so the error image is part of the evidence. A smooth wall and a box silhouette can have the same average contribution to a global metric while requiring very different reconstruction policies. Adaptive probe placement, disocclusion handling, and normal/depth compatibility are responses to those local failures.

Unreal's screen-probe parameters include separate tracing and gathering resolutions, atlas sizes, downsampling, interpolation depth weights, and importance-sampling resources. A directional tracing layout need not be identical to a filtered gathering layout. The source supports spherical-harmonic or octahedral irradiance formats and specialized integration classifications. Those choices preserve different amounts of directional information and permit different consumers; they should be selected after defining what the later shading pass needs. There is another optimization: use screen depth to test a ray before tracing the scene representation. Project points along the ray into the previous or current view, traverse the depth hierarchy, and look for a geometrically plausible depth crossing. A valid screen hit can exploit detailed visible geometry. A ray that leaves the image, passes behind an ambiguous depth layer, or finds no trustworthy crossing must remain unresolved for a fallback. The depth buffer contains one visible layer; it cannot certify the absence of an off-screen occluder.

Replace the diffuse hemisphere with a rough reflection lobe

The tall box now becomes reflective. A perfectly smooth mirror chooses one reflected direction; a rough surface spreads reflection over a lobe. A diffuse irradiance value cannot represent both. It has already integrated away the direction information needed to ask what the mirror sees. Reflection therefore needs directional samples, appropriate PDFs, and a geometry-and-lighting lookup for each chosen ray.

Use outgoing view direction \(v\) and incoming sampled direction \(l\), both pointing away from the surface. Their normalized half-vector is \(h=(v+l)/\|v+l\|\). For isotropic GGX with width \(\alpha>0\), define \(c_h=n\cdot h\):

\[ D(h)=\frac{\alpha^2}{\pi\left[1+(\alpha^2-1)c_h^2\right]^2}, \qquad f_r(v,l)=\frac{D(h)F(v\cdot h)G(v,l)}{4(n\cdot v)(n\cdot l)}. \]

The normal distribution \(D\) is normalized with projected area: \(\int D(h)(n\cdot h)\,d\omega_h=1\). Thus sampling \(p_h=D(h)c_h\) samples microfacet normals, not reflected rays. In polar coordinates, integrate the density after azimuthal integration. With \(s=\cos^2\theta\) and \(ds=-2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta\), the resulting cumulative distribution is:

\[ F_\theta(\theta)=\frac{\sin^2\theta}{\alpha^2\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta}. \quad u=F_\theta(\theta)\Rightarrow \cos\theta=\sqrt{\frac{1-u}{1+(\alpha^2-1)u}}, \quad \phi=2\pi u_2. \]

Reflect the view direction about the sampled normal: \(l=2(v\cdot h)h-v\). The reflection transformation has solid-angle Jacobian \(d\omega_l=4|v\cdot h|\,d\omega_h\). One way to see the factor is to orient polar coordinates around \(v\): the half-vector polar angle is half the reflected-direction polar angle, so \(\sin(2\theta_h)\,2d\theta_h/(\sin\theta_h\,d\theta_h)=4\cos\theta_h\). Consequently:

\[ p_l(l)=\frac{D(h)(n\cdot h)}{4|v\cdot h|}, \qquad \widehat L_{\rm specular}=\frac1N\sum_j \frac{L_i(l_j)f_r(v,l_j)(n\cdot l_j)}{p_l(l_j)}. \]

Substitution cancels \(D\) and the factor four. For accepted directions above the surface, each sample weight becomes \(F G |v\cdot h|/[(n\cdot v)(n\cdot h)]\). Samples below the surface contribute zero. They must not be discarded and replaced without also changing the probability model, or the estimator becomes incorrectly conditioned on successful samples.

The lab sets \(\alpha=r^2\), with artist-facing roughness \(r\). It uses separable Smith masking \(G=G_1(v)G_1(l)\), where \(G_1(c)=2c/[c+\sqrt{\alpha^2+(1-\alpha^2)c^2}]\) for positive cosine, and a Schlick Fresnel approximation \(F=F_0+(1-F_0)(1-v\cdot h)^5\). The displayed object uses neutral \(F_0=0.75\) and a small diffuse visual contribution. This is a rough reflective teaching material, not a fitted Unreal material or a complete energy-compensated multilobe BSDF.

At grazing angles, sampling the full normal distribution wastes many normals hidden from the view. Visible-normal sampling instead uses \(p_h^{\rm vis}=D(h)G_1(v)\max(0,v\cdot h)/(n\cdot v)\) over visible microfacets. Applying the same reflection Jacobian gives \(p_l^{\rm vis}=D(h)G_1(v)/[4(n\cdot v)]\). The estimator weight simplifies to \(FG/G_1(v)\), or \(FG_1(l)\) for separable Smith masking. This cancellation reduces high weights and rejected work. Heitz's visible-GGX paper provides a practical construction for sampling the distribution.

The distinction is visible in real code: the inspected Lumen shader calls ImportanceSampleVisibleGGX, transforms the sampled half-vector through the tangent basis, and reflects the camera vector. Material anisotropy can modify its directional roughness. The reference deliberately uses the easier full-NDF sampling derivation above; it must not be described as an exact copy of that Lumen shader. Both need correct ray PDFs, surface visibility, and a lighting evaluation policy. Lumen's visible-normal sampling path.

Finally, distinguish tracing geometry from shading the hit. A hardware ray can intersect an accurate triangle and still receive approximate lighting from a surface cache. Evaluating lighting at the hit can restore view-dependent material response that a diffuse cache cannot supply, at additional cost. Unreal exposes reflection hit-lighting choices for this reason. Our reference uses exact rectangles for intersection and approximate diffuse cached radiance at reflection hits: accurate intersection alone does not make the reflected image a fully path-traced solution.

Reuse history only after proving it still describes the same surface

Low ray counts produce noise. Temporal reuse replaces a single estimate with a history of estimates. For a stationary signal and independent samples, an exponential moving average obeys \(H_t=(1-\alpha)H_{t-1}+\alpha X_t\), where \(0<\alpha\le1\). Unrolling it separates initialization from observation weights:

\[ H_t=(1-\alpha)^tH_0+ \alpha\sum_{k=1}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{t-k}X_k. \]

If every estimate has expectation \(\mu\), the mean error is \((1-\alpha)^t(H_0-\mu)\). With independent noise variance \(\sigma^2\), the steady-state variance sums a geometric series:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(H_\infty) =\alpha^2\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-\alpha)^{2j} =\frac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}\sigma^2. \]

Lower alpha reduces noise but delays response. After a signal changes from \(\mu_0\) to \(\mu_1\), expected remaining error after \(k\) updates is \((1-\alpha)^k(\mu_0-\mu_1)\). This is a predictable bias from stale information. Correlated temporal samples add covariance terms to the variance expression; the independent-sample formula is not a universal performance guarantee.

Before blending, establish correspondence. A camera translation moves a surface to a different pixel. The lab moves the camera 45 centimeters sideways, reconstructs each new hit point, projects it into the old camera, and samples the old surface data there. History is accepted only if the old image covers that projection, the exact planar face identity agrees, reconstructed world positions agree within eight centimeters, and normal dot product exceeds 0.95. Those are deliberately visible lab thresholds, not Unreal configuration defaults.

Face identity matters in this scene: the emitter lies only two centimeters below the ceiling and has the same normal direction. Position and normal tolerances alone accepted 28 ceiling-history samples for newly visible emitter pixels. The corrected test rejects those cross-face matches and asserts that none survive. For dynamic geometry, a face index alone would also need stable identity and compatible geometry/material revisions.

A valid projection is not sufficient if the scene itself changes. Moving an object can preserve a screen location while changing the hit identity. Changing the light preserves geometry while invalidating lighting. Changing exposure can change stored numerical values without changing physical radiance. Material versions, geometry versions, velocity, depth, normals, hit distance, and pre-exposure all participate in the appropriate history policy. Different caches need different invalidation scopes.

The inspected screen-probe filtering shader carries previous probe depth, radiance, and translated world position; it reconstructs a history screen position and applies bounds and geometric checks before gathering history. The reference's simple tests demonstrate the reason for those resources. They are not an exhaustive transcription of Lumen's temporal filter, reflection disocclusion treatment, or variance-based clamping. Screen-probe temporal resources and reprojection.

The correct fallback is an explicit low-confidence current estimate. Zeroing all lighting may produce a dark flash; keeping invalid history produces ghosting. A renderer can spend extra rays on disoccluded regions, temporarily use nearby compatible estimates, or accept more noise for a few frames. Each policy trades a visible error against work. Treating validity as data makes that trade explicit instead of burying it in a smoothing constant.

The script's order is the implementation order of the reduced renderer. face() establishes explicit geometry and material ownership. intersect() provides exact nearest-hit queries. Face-local texel generation creates the cache parameterization. direct() evaluates the area emitter and shadow segments. Cosine rays build the sparse visibility matrix. Repeated sparse matrix multiplication solves the sampled diffuse transport. lookup() reconstructs surface radiance from the texels. These operations do not rely on the plotting code.

The SDF diagnostic is independent of the transport backend so that it can be compared with exact intersections without contaminating the lighting reference. Likewise, the world-probe interpolation comparison and the screen-probe gather are separate consumers of the solved surface field. The reference does not compose world-probe fallback into screen-probe rays. It demonstrates both representations and their errors independently. That boundary is intentional and visible in the code; combining them requires a defined coverage interval and a rule preventing near-field light from being counted twice.

Replace one subsystem at a time. Substitute a triangle BVH for rectangle intersections and compare ray hits. Substitute a sampled SDF for the analytic field and compare against that BVH. Replace fixed planar cards with generated orthographic captures and inspect coverage before lighting them. Move the transport gathers to compute shaders while preserving linear-array outputs for comparison. Use measurements to decide what the implementation has actually earned

The downloadable metrics file records the seed, array sizes, convergence history, trace agreement, and reconstruction error from this run. The script asserts that all tested SDF rays agree on visibility, the common-hit distance error stays below one millimeter, spherical quadrature weights sum to \(4\pi\), and the diffuse solve remains finite and nonnegative. Its random seed is fixed at 20261015; the sampler validation uses its own independent fixed seed.

These checks support specific conclusions. The analytic SDF marcher agrees with exact geometry on the tested ray family. The angular weights cover a sphere correctly. The finite sampled transport operator is contractive in this scene and converges. Screen-probe reconstruction has measurable error relative to the fine surface-cache lookup. None of these checks proves that the educational renderer reproduces Unreal Lumen or that a final image is an unbiased ground-truth solution.

When extending the renderer, add adversarial scenes before adding polish. A thin wall between two differently lit rooms tests visibility leakage. A bright emitter revealed by a camera turn tests PDF support and cache freshness. A glossy surface reflecting an off-screen moving object tests the separation of primary visibility and reflection geometry. A material change without movement tests capture invalidation. A camera teleport tests residency and disocclusion. A light switched off tests whether temporal reuse decays stale energy at the intended rate.

The essential implementation contract is now concrete. Geometry queries return hit identity and distance. Material captures describe that hit independently of the current camera. Surface lighting updates solve a discretized transport problem. Directional probes reuse expensive radiance queries over space. Screen reconstruction respects surfaces instead of blindly filtering pixels. Reflection sampling keeps the BRDF and its PDF consistent. Temporal reuse carries evidence that old and new samples correspond. That is a system a programmer can build, measure, and gradually specialize into a real-time renderer.
